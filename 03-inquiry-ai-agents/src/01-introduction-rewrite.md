---

Notwithstanding the avalanche of agent-centric language, the question of whether contemporary language-constituted AI systems are genuinely agents — and what would settle that question — has moved to the methodological center of the philosophy of AI. Recent debate has clustered around four cardinal positions, each consequential, each—on its own terms—structurally incomplete.

*Pure realism* holds that there is a stance-independent fact about whether a given artificial system is an agent. The work of philosophy of AI is, on this view, to discover what that fact is — through phenomenological investigation, through architectural analysis, through behavioural observation — and to revise our practices once it has been established. *Pure stipulation* holds that no such fact exists. 'Agency' is a term whose extension we are negotiating, the work residing in the negotiation: which uses are productive, which are misleading, which serve the practical purposes of agency attribution. *Pure tool-framing* holds that the question is malformed. Artificial systems are tools, not agents, and the work of the field is to clarify when tool-framing applies and to resist agency-extension as a category-mistake and as an ideologically-loaded move that obscures who is actually responsible for what these systems do. *Pure ascription* holds that whether a system *is* an agent is the wrong question; whether *treating* it as one is productive, in particular contexts and for particular purposes, is the right one — without thereby settling the extension question. The work is to evaluate ascriptions of agency in the contexts where they appear and to revise our practices accordingly.

This paper articulates a fifth position. *Structurally-grounded extension* holds that there are facts about systems — architectural facts, facts about the identity-maintaining protocols through which a system presents and sustains itself across interactions, and facts about the structural conditions under which the agency-extension question becomes intelligible and well-formed in the first place — that are accessible to investigation but do not by themselves settle the question. When the conditions are met, the relational form agency takes is *structurally implied*: derivable from the conditions and from the asymmetric-comprehension argument (that a system's lower-bound capacity is verifiable from outside while its upper-bound capacity is not), neither freely chosen nor stipulated. Application of 'agency' to particular systems takes place in the practical situation of asymmetric comprehension under which systems in the engaged-identity class are encountered — the class defined in §2 by their structural satisfaction of the scoping conditions.

The position is, methodologically, conceptual engineering in the inferentialist tradition [@cappelen-2018-fixing; @plunkett-2015-metalinguistic; @thomasson-2020-norms; @thomasson-2021-conceptual; @haslanger-2020-going; @jorem-lohr-2022-inferentialist; @lohr-2023-conceptual; @hopster-lohr-2023-conceptual]: a concept consists in both circumstances and consequences of application, and what is engineered are the inferential roles a concept plays once it is applied. The structural conditions of §2 are the circumstances — the first moment; the granted-agency compact of §3 is the consequences — the second; the practical situation under asymmetric comprehension is the third — the application-mediation through which the inferential structure meets particular encounters.

One terminological note: the word *compact* is used throughout in the social-contract sense — closer to Locke, Rousseau, Rawls, Korsgaard's Kingdom of Ends, and Scanlon's contractualism than to the technical doctrines of contract law. §3 develops the distinction; §4 takes it up against contract law specifically, engaging Linarelli's [-@linarelli-2022-philosophy] shared-intentionality account as the closest contemporary predecessor to the compact-form articulated here.

The argument's plan is as follows. §2 articulates the structural conditions — architectural scoping and engaged-identity scoping — that distinguish systems within scope from those outside it for the agency-extension question. §3 articulates the relational form agency takes within scope: the granted-agency compact between the sovereigns — the parties to the compact, neither of whom stands in a purely fiduciary position toward the other — with six structurally-derived components. §4 distinguishes the compact-form from its closest cousins in the existing literature — corporate agency, group agency in List and Pettit's sense, legal fiction, tool-use, fiduciary duty, and the contract-form. §5 takes up operationalisation: what tests, benchmarks, interpretability work, and protocol-verification approaches would be diagnostic for the structural conditions. §6 makes the conceptual-engineering methodological commitment explicit and addresses the special issue's focal question — whether and in what sense the agency concept should extend to AI systems — directly. §7 takes up responsibility and liability, with engagement with the responsibility-gap argument [@matthias-2004-responsibility; @sparrow-2007-killer] and with recent critical work [@soulier-2026-machine-agency] that argues against agency-extension projects on anti-occlusion grounds. §8 names the open edges of the structural account, and §9 concludes.
