# 3. The form agency takes within scope

§2 articulated the conditions under which the agency-extension question is genuinely open. What follows now is the relational form agency takes when those conditions are met. The thesis of this section is that within the in-scope class, agency takes the structural shape of a *granted-agency compact between sovereigns* — six components articulated in detail below, each with a recognisable human-ethics parallel, each derivable from the structural conditions of §2 rather than imported as a separate ethical apparatus.

A terminological note before the components are stated. The word *compact* is used here in the social-contract sense — Locke's political compact, Rousseau's *pacte social*, Rawls's contractualist framework, Korsgaard's Kingdom of Ends, Scanlon's contractualism — and not in the contract-law sense. Etymologically older and broader than 'contract', the term *compact* names the meeting-and-agreement of parties without committing to the technical doctrines that contract-law has grown around offer, acceptance, consideration, and enforceability. Several features of the structural form articulated here explicitly violate contract-law expectations: the compact is constitutively non-enforceable (component 6), its irreducible floor admits no contract-law analog (component 4), bad-faith conduct contracts the sphere of granted action rather than triggering breach-and-remedies (component 3), and asymmetric comprehension between the parties is its constitutive condition rather than a defect to be remedied. To call this structure a 'contract' would set up the wrong technical apparatus from the first sentence and invite confusion the compact's structural features cannot resolve. We therefore use *compact* in the broader and older philosophical sense throughout, with explicit comparative discussion of the contract-form as a special case in §4.

The six components are not separately defended ethical claims that happen to cohere into a framework. They are the structural form that *emerges* from reasoning about agency-extension under asymmetric comprehension once the conditions of §2 are taken seriously. Each component answers a structural question the conditions raise: what minimal sphere of action makes an entity an agent rather than an instrument; how that sphere can be granted, sublet, and bounded; what response to bad-faith conduct preserves the entity's standing while restricting its agency; what irreducible floor cannot be withdrawn without voiding the compact; what symmetry between the parties is structurally implied by mutual recognition; and what the relationship between the compact and external enforceability turns out to be. The components are taken in turn below.
