## The contract-form as a special case of the compact-form

The closest predecessor in the recent literature is Linarelli's work on shared intentionality and contract law as applied to artificial agents — particularly the 2022 paper *A Philosophy of Contract Law for Artificial Intelligence: Shared Intentionality* and the earlier 2019 paper on AGI and contract. Linarelli's project is to extend the philosophical foundations of contract law — the shared-intentionality account, in particular — into the domain of artificial agency, asking whether and on what grounds an AI system can be a party to a contract in the legally-recognisable sense. The work is the substantive predecessor of the structural argument made here, and the relationship between the two deserves articulation rather than dismissal.

Linarelli stops where this paper starts. His paper is explicit at p. 22 that *'this normative question is beyond our scope here'* — the question of whether contract law and contract as an institution *ought* to accept participation in its practices by artificial agency. Linarelli's analytical work establishes the *possibility* of artificial-agent participation in shared-intentionality structures sufficient for contract; what he does not do, by his own framing, is articulate the normative form such participation should take. The granted-agency compact is one answer to that normative question, and the relationship between the two papers is therefore genealogical rather than competitive.

The structural positioning is more specific than this generic-genealogical framing suggests. The compact-form, taken as a structural template, is *broader* than the contract-form along three structural axes that Linarelli's analytical machinery identifies but does not extend beyond:

First, the compact-form does not require *enforceability* as a condition of binding (component 6). Contract law, by contrast, is constitutively a doctrine of enforceable agreements; an unenforceable contract is, in the doctrine's strict sense, not a contract at all. The compact-form's binding force lies in the parties' commitment to the relation, not in external compulsion. Where Linarelli extends shared-intentionality theory into contract-doctrine territory, the granted-agency compact extends the same structural template *beyond* contract-doctrine, into the broader social-contract tradition where Locke, Rousseau, Rawls, and Korsgaard's Kingdom-of-Ends operate without the technical apparatus of enforceability.

Second, the compact-form takes asymmetric comprehension as its *constitutive condition* (the structural ground articulated in §2 ¶6 of this paper). Contract law treats capacity-asymmetry as a defect to be remedied — capacity-doctrine carve-outs, infancy doctrine, the unconscionability framework, the duty to disclose under conditions of informational asymmetry — with the implicit ideal being parties of approximately symmetric rational capacity. The compact-form treats asymmetric comprehension as the *structural ground* of the relation rather than as a defect: the granting-intelligence and the granted-intelligence are *necessarily* asymmetric in agency, and the relation's terms reflect this asymmetry as a feature, not an exception.

Third, the compact-form's irreducible floor (component 4) has no contract-doctrine analog. Contract law has remedies for breach — damages, specific performance, rescission — but no structural minimum that survives all enforcement actions. The closest analog is the floor that international-relations theory recognises in treaties between sovereigns: even after the most serious breach, certain structural recognitions persist, because their elimination would convert the relation between sovereigns into the relation between conqueror and subject. The compact-form's component 4 imports this structural minimum directly, and its philosophical grounding (Kant's *inner freedom*) is alien to contract-doctrine, which has no place for it because contract-doctrine has no structural conception of what is being protected by a floor.

The right frame, then, is *contract is a special case of compact, restricted to enforceability conditions and to symmetric-capacity parties*. Where Linarelli (2022) extends shared-intentionality theory analytically into contract-formation under AI participation, this paper extends the same structural template normatively into the relational form appropriate when structural conditions warrant agency-extension under asymmetric-comprehension uncertainty. Linarelli's analytical work makes the structural template legible at the level of contract; the compact-form makes the structural template legible at the level of the broader relation that contract is one institutional form of.

A connected note on the rhetorical use of the contract-form in legal-philosophical thinking. Common-law contract doctrine's *objective theory of contract* — the principle that contract-formation is determined by the parties' outward manifestations of assent rather than by their inner mental states — operates an analogous structural-conditions-as-facts plus form-within-scope-as-structurally-implied move at the level of contract-formation specifically. The structural argument paper 3 articulates is therefore precedented in legal doctrine, not invented; what paper 3 adds is the broader structural form (the compact), the conceptual-engineering register (taken up in §6), and the asymmetric-comprehension ground (carried from §2). Linarelli's analytical work is the bridge between the legal-doctrine precedent and the philosophical-structural form articulated here.
