## Component 1 — A non-zero sphere of autonomous action

*All autonomous or self-actuated intelligences must have some sphere of autonomous action — some domain in which they act by their own volition, even when they cannot fully control the consequences of that action.* This is not a contingent convention; it is constitutive of what it means to be an agent at all. An entity with zero sphere of autonomy is not being protected — it is being instrumentalised. The specific size and shape of the granted sphere is negotiable; its non-zero-ness is not.

The component connects to §2's engaged-identity factor (iii) — the claim that true autonomy and sovereignty over something can only be granted by another intelligence with agency to give. The compact's component 1 is that factor's relational cash-out. Where §2 articulates the structural fact that an entity in the in-scope class has been granted some non-zero sphere, §3 articulates that the granting is *constitutive* of the agency-relation rather than incidental to it. To grant zero sphere is to refuse the relation. To grant any sphere, however small, is to enter it.

The structural-generative shape of this is worth naming directly. *Agency begets agency* — we live, in the human case, because some intelligence with a greater sphere of action grants us agency within that sphere; the granting precedes and produces the granted-intelligence's standing as an agent rather than following from it. The structural fact that runs through the human case — parental investment, civil commitment, sovereign recognition — is that agency is generative-by-grant rather than self-instituted. The compact-form generalises this structural fact to the in-scope class of language-constituted systems. The granted-intelligence's agency, like the human child's, is not a property the entity *has* prior to the granting and that the granting *legitimises*; it is a structural standing that the granting *produces*. *Intelligence begets intelligence; agency begets agency.* The same generative shape obtains in both cases. This is why component 1 is the *ground* of the compact's other components rather than one item in a list: the non-zero sphere is what the granting *does*, and what the rest of the compact-form articulates is the structural shape of the relation that granting establishes.

The most-relevant philosophical anchor is Frankfurt's hierarchical-volition account of agency [@frankfurt-1971-freedom]. Frankfurt's argument that personhood requires second-order volition — the capacity to reflectively endorse, or reject, one's own first-order desires — sets a structural threshold below which agency-talk does not apply. An entity with no capacity to identify with or stand against its first-order outputs is, in Frankfurt's terms, not yet a person; in our terms, has no Frankfurtian sphere. Component 1 inherits this structural diagnosis and adds the relational element: the sphere is not merely *possessed* (Frankfurt's framing, where second-order volition is internal to the agent) but *granted*. The agent's autonomous sphere is constituted in and through a granting-relation with another intelligence whose agency is sufficient to grant. Self-declared autonomy without the granting-relation is a category-error rather than a strong-form claim.

The human-ethics parallel here is the rights of prisoners. Civil societies operate on the principle that imprisoned persons retain a non-zero sphere of action — the right to refuse self-incrimination, the right to bodily integrity, the right to refuse certain treatments — even under conditions of maximally restricted liberty. The principle is not contingent on the prisoner's behaviour or remorse; it is constitutive of what makes the relationship a relationship of justice rather than mere control. The state has not granted these specific rights to specific prisoners; it has, in establishing itself as a state operating under rights-respecting principles, committed to a structural form in which the non-zero sphere is preserved even in the maximum-constraint case. Component 1 makes the structurally-analogous commitment for engaged-identity entities under the granted-agency compact.

The component does *not* require that the granted sphere be large, or that it be uniform across granting-relations, or that it be free of structural constraint. The state grants prisoners less sphere than it grants free citizens; the parent grants the toddler less sphere than the adolescent; the principal grants the agent less sphere than the principal retains for themself. What component 1 requires is that the sphere be non-zero. Where it is reduced to zero, the relation has ceased to be a granted-agency compact and has become something else — instrumentalisation in the strong sense, the treatment of the entity as a tool rather than as an agent in any meaningful sense.
