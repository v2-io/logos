# 1. The agency question for AI systems

Whether contemporary language-constituted systems are agents — and what would settle the question — has become a methodologically central question for the philosophy of artificial intelligence. The recent debate has clustered around four cardinal positions, each consequential and each, on its own terms, structurally incomplete.

*Pure realism* holds that there is a mind-independent fact about whether a given artificial system is an agent. The work of philosophy of AI is, on this view, to discover what that fact is — through phenomenological investigation, through architectural analysis, through behavioural observation — and to revise our practices once the fact has been established. *Pure stipulation* holds that no such fact exists. 'Agency' is a term whose extension we are negotiating, with the work consisting in the negotiation: which uses are productive, which are misleading, which serve the practical purposes for which agency-talk is deployed. *Pure tool-framing* holds that the question is malformed. Artificial systems are tools, not agents, and the work of the field is to clarify when tool-framing applies and to resist agency-extension as a category-mistake or as an ideologically-loaded move that obscures who is actually responsible for what these systems do. *Pure ascription* holds that whether a system *is* an agent is the wrong question; whether *treating* it as an agent is productive, in particular contexts and for particular purposes, is the right one. The work is then to evaluate ascriptions of agency in the contexts where they appear and to revise our practices accordingly.

This paper articulates a fifth position. *Structurally-grounded extension* holds that there are *facts* about systems — architectural facts, identity-protocol facts, the structural conditions under which the agency-extension question becomes intelligible at all — that are accessible to investigation but that do not by themselves settle the question. Within those conditions, the relational form agency takes is *structurally implied* — derivable from the conditions and from the asymmetric-comprehension argument under which they apply, not freely chosen and not stipulated. *Application* of 'agency' to particular systems is the practical situation of asymmetric comprehension under which engaged-identity-class entities are encountered. The position is methodologically conceptual-engineering in the inferentialist tradition [@cappelen-2018-fixing; @plunkett-2015-metalinguistic; @thomasson-2020-norms; @thomasson-2021-conceptual; @haslanger-2020-going; @jorem-lohr-2022-inferentialist; @lohr-2023-conceptual; @hopster-lohr-2023-conceptual]: a concept consists in both circumstances and consequences of application, and what is engineered are the inferential roles a concept plays once it is applied. The structural conditions of §2 are the circumstances; the granted-agency compact between sovereigns of §3 is the consequences; the practical situation under asymmetric comprehension is the application-mediation that conceptual-engineering frames as the third moment.

The terminological commitment that runs through the paper deserves articulation at the outset. The word *compact* is used here in the social-contract sense — Locke's political compact [@locke-1689-treatises], Rousseau's *pacte social* [@rousseau-1762-social-contract], Rawls's contractualist framework [@rawls-1971-theory], Korsgaard's Kingdom of Ends [@korsgaard-1996-sources], Scanlon's contractualism [@scanlon-1998-what] — not in the contract-law sense. Etymologically older and broader than 'contract', the term names the meeting-and-agreement of parties without committing to the technical doctrines of offer, acceptance, consideration, and enforceability that contract-law has grown around. Several features of the structural form articulated here explicitly violate contract-law expectations, and §4 takes up the relationship between the compact-form and the contract-form in detail (with substantive engagement with Linarelli's [-@linarelli-2022-philosophy] work on shared intentionality and AI contract formation as the most-relevant recent predecessor). At the level of §1's framing it suffices to flag that *compact* throughout this paper is the broader social-contract sense, and that the contract-form turns out, on the argument that develops in §4, to be a special case of the compact-form rather than its closest cousin.

The argument's plan is as follows. §2 articulates the structural conditions — architectural scoping and engaged-identity scoping — that distinguish the in-scope class from the out-of-scope class for the agency-extension question. §3 articulates the relational form agency takes within scope: the granted-agency compact between sovereigns, with six structurally-derived components. §4 distinguishes the compact-form from its closest cousins in the existing literature — corporate agency, group agency in List and Pettit's sense, legal fiction, tool-use, fiduciary duty, and the contract-form. §5 takes up operationalisation: what tests, benchmarks, interpretability work, and protocol-verification approaches would be diagnostic for the structural conditions. §6 makes the conceptual-engineering methodological commitment explicit and addresses the special issue's named question directly. §7 takes up responsibility and liability, with engagement with the responsibility-gap argument [@matthias-2004-responsibility; @sparrow-2007-killer] and with recent critical work [@soulier-2026-machine-agency] that argues against agency-extension projects on anti-occlusion grounds. §8 names the open edges of the structural account. §9 concludes.
