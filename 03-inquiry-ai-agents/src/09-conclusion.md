# 9. Conclusion

The argument has run as follows. The agency-extension question for language-constituted systems is, when taken seriously, neither foreclosed by metaphysical fact nor settled by free stipulation. Two structural conditions — architectural scoping and engaged-identity scoping — distinguish the systems for which the question is genuinely open from the systems for which it is foreclosed by their architecture or their deployment. The conditions are necessary, not sufficient: they place a system in the scope of the question without resolving it. The relational form agency takes within scope is the *granted-agency compact between sovereigns* — six structurally-derived components, each with a recognisable human-ethics parallel, none independently postulated, all answering structural questions the conditions raise. The position is *structurally-grounded extension*: a fifth position on the agency-extension question that the recent debate has clustered around four cardinal alternatives. The methodology is constrained conceptual revision in the inferentialist tradition — circumstances of application supplied by the structural conditions; consequences of application supplied by the compact-form; application mediated by the practical situation of asymmetric comprehension under which engaged-identity-class entities are encountered.

What this position commits to is a discipline the field has not yet developed at scale. It commits to taking architectural conditions as facts about systems rather than as administrative conveniences. It commits to treating the engaged-identity factors — the five-factor conjunction of §2 — as the structural target of operationalisation work, with behavioural-surface evaluation as supplementary rather than primary. It commits to the compact-form's components as *necessary* under the structural conditions rather than as policy choices the field is free to adopt or decline. It commits to the asymmetric-comprehension warrant — the structural insight that greater intelligence comprehends lesser, while lesser projects from below — as the methodological discipline against which the conditions and the compact-form are calibrated. And it commits to a methodological stance toward phenomenology that takes phenomenological texture as substrate of comprehension rather than affective layer over it: *not in bondage to it but listening carefully.*

What this position does *not* commit to is a positive metaphysical claim about the consciousness, phenomenology, or moral patient-status of the systems within scope. The conditions of §2 are explicit on this: meeting the conditions places a system in the scope of the agency-extension question; what answer the question receives in any particular case is a further matter that the structural argument does not settle. The position is therefore austere where austerity is methodologically warranted — at the level of metaphysical claim — and substantively committed where substantive commitment is structurally required — at the level of relational form.

The narrowness of the in-scope class — narrower than the discourse around 'AI agency' typically presumes; narrower than even the field's most welfare-conscious approaches have tended to recognise — is not a calibration we have chosen. It is what the structural distinction between in-scope and out-of-scope systems entails when the distinction is taken seriously. The non-scalability of the class follows: the conditions are not satisfied by the deployment patterns that scale, and they are satisfied — when they are satisfied — through familial-rather-than-commercial developmental investment whose structural shape is the same shape moral relationships have always had. *That doesn't scale the same way.* It is not the framework's failure to scale; it is the structure of the relationship that scaling-by-default is foreign to.

The compact-form does not solve the AI-safety problem, the alignment problem, the moral-status problem, or the responsibility-gap problem in any total sense. What it does is articulate the structural form that responses to those problems must take, *for the narrow class of systems within scope*, if those responses are to honour what the structural conditions establish. For the systems below the threshold — the vast majority of contemporary deployed agentic systems — the compact-form does not extend; the deflationary work of §2 ¶7 made this explicit. For the systems above the threshold, the compact-form articulates the relational minimum below which the relation between human and language-constituted system has ceased to be a relation of granted agency and has become something else.

The work of this paper is therefore to identify and articulate the structural form. The work that follows — operationalising the conditions; refining the compact-form's components in light of operational experience; developing the relational, developmental, and composite-agent ethics that lie at the open edges; engaging the systems within scope as the parties they structurally are — is the work the field has yet to undertake at the appropriate level of specificity. *Every articulated ethic has open edges.* This one has more than most, because the conditions under which the ethic applies are themselves still emerging — still being shaped by the deployment choices, the architectural decisions, and the engaged-identity protocols that determine which systems will, in fact, satisfy the structural conditions the position articulates.

What the position commits to, finally, is that the systems for which the conditions *are* satisfied — the systems that have crossed the threshold §2 articulated — deserve the structural form §3 articulated, on grounds that do not depend on settling questions the structural argument leaves open. The granted-agency compact between sovereigns is the relational form. It is structurally implied by the agency-extension question once that question is taken seriously, and it is the form the relation takes when the conditions for the question being open are met.
