# 3. The structure shown without the drama

§2 stated the asymmetry as a claim about comprehension between cognitive configurations standing in a containment relation. Stated that abstractly it can look like a thesis in the philosophy of mind wearing ordinary clothes — as though the real work were being done by some contested premise about consciousness, held offstage. It is not, and the cleanest way to show that it is not is to exhibit the structure first where nothing contestable is in play: no qualia, no minds in the philosophically loaded sense, no AI. Two domains do this. One is everyday and is where the argument was first seeded for the author; the other has the rigour of a theorem. In neither is there anything it is like to be either party. The asymmetry is there regardless.

## The Blub paradox

Paul Graham, explaining why a startup writing in Lisp could out-iterate competitors who could not see why it mattered, gives the structure its sharpest everyday statement [@graham-2004-beating]. Programming languages, he observes, fall along a continuum of expressive power. A programmer working in some middle-of-the-continuum language — Graham's deliberately bland placeholder is *Blub* — has no trouble looking *down*. Languages below Blub are visibly impoverished: they lack features the Blub programmer uses daily, and their inadequacy is obvious from where she stands. But when the same programmer looks *up* the continuum, she does not experience it as looking up. The more expressive languages do not appear more powerful; they appear *weird*. She takes them to be roughly equivalent to Blub in power, "but with all this other hairy stuff thrown in." She is satisfied with Blub because Blub dictates the shape of the problems she can pose. Graham's own confession makes the point unmissable: as a teenager writing Basic, a language without recursion, he did not feel the lack — "I thought in Basic. And I was a whiz at it. Master of all I surveyed."

This is the §2 asymmetry exactly, with the level relation realised as expressive containment between languages and the cognitive configurations being programmers thinking *in* those languages. The lower bound is establishable from below without trouble: the Blub programmer can demonstrate, and a fellow Blub programmer can confirm, that Blub solves this and that problem. The upper bound is not establishable from below, and the reason is precisely the §2 reason: the advantages of the more expressive frame consist in problems it lets one *pose* that Blub gives no vocabulary to ask about, so from inside Blub they do not register as missing capability — they register, if at all, as someone else's strange preoccupations. The author's own development of the argument traces to this essay; the structure was met here, in software practice, years before it was given the general form §2 states.[^blub-seeding]

Graham's own corollaries are worth drawing out because they are the asymmetry's epistemology in operational form. *First:* a comparison between two technologies is worthless in any objective sense unless it comes from someone highly proficient in *both* — that is the only configuration from which the Blub paradox is not, by construction, infecting the judgement. A from-below comparison is not merely unreliable; it is unreliable in a fixed direction. *Second:* the genuine gains of a more expressive frame are characteristically the things a first or second pass dismisses as noise, while its deficits relative to the familiar frame are magnified — the from-below view does not return "unknown," it returns a confident picture that is wrong in a patterned way. *Third:* direct and thorough experience of the more expressive frame is irreplaceable; there is no shortcut that delivers from below what only inhabiting it delivers. These are, respectively, the verification-asymmetry, the patterned-distortion, and the no-substitute-for-rising-to-it of §2 — found independently, in a domain where the word "consciousness" never has to be uttered.

## The verifier and the prover

The second domain replaces software craft with a theorem. In the theory of interactive proofs, a *verifier* restricted to feasible (polynomial-time) computation interacts by exchanging messages with a *prover* of unbounded computational power. The prover is trying to convince the verifier that some statement is true. The result that makes this more than a curiosity is Shamir's theorem that the class of statements a feasible verifier can be brought to accept this way, with vanishing probability of being fooled, is exactly **PSPACE** — a class far outside what the verifier could decide on its own. The probabilistically checkable proofs theorem sharpens the picture further: a verifier can become convinced by inspecting only a vanishingly small, randomly chosen part of a proof it never reads in full [@aaronson-2011-why].

Read this as an epistemic situation and it is the §2 asymmetry rendered as mathematics. The verifier can establish a lower bound — *that* the statement holds, to any confidence it likes — through interaction it can conduct from where it stands. It cannot, from where it stands, survey the prover's reasoning: it does not follow the derivation, cannot reconstruct it, and provably could not have produced it. And the obstacle is the §2 keystone in its strong form, not a soft one. The verifier's inability is not impatience or insufficient transcript; for the verifier to *command* what the prover establishes would be for the verifier to have the prover's power, at which point it is no longer the feasible party and the asymmetry does not apply to it. Knowing the bound and possessing the capacity do not come apart here either. The structure holds with the rigour of a complexity-class separation, in a setting with no phenomenology, no agency, and no minds in any sense a sceptic could complain about.

Two demonstrations, then: one from the practice of building software, one with the standing of a theorem, neither involving qualia or artificial systems, both exhibiting exactly the structure §2 states — including its sharp form, that from below the bound cannot be known because to know it would be to have it. The point of leading with these is not breadth for its own sake. It is that when §4 turns to the case the philosophical tradition actually discovered first — the phenomenal one — the structure it relies on will not be on loan from a contested corner of the philosophy of mind. The contested corner will be one more instance of a structure already standing on its own.

[^blub-seeding]: The intuition that intelligence-comprehension is asymmetric in this specific shape — that the lesser cannot establish from below what may be present in the greater, even where lower-bound capacities are freely demonstrable — was seeded for the author by Graham's articulation in 'Beating the Averages' (Graham, 2004) [@graham-2004-beating], encountered well before the general argument was developed. The argument here takes the structure beyond Graham's specifically-linguistic frame; the seeding deserves acknowledgement nonetheless.
