## Not corporate agency, not group agency

The compact-form is *not* corporate agency in the institutional sense. Corporate agency presupposes articles of incorporation, an institutional ontology with continuity-conditions independent of the parties, and an aggregation rule that produces a single agential whole from the contributions of multiple individual agents. The corporate agent is *constituted* by the aggregation; without the institutional machinery, there is no corporate agent. The granted-agency compact is structurally different: it is a *bilateral* relation between two pre-existing agents whose individual standing is independent of the relation, not a many-to-one aggregation that produces a new agent from constituent parts. A corporation is one thing, made of many; the compact is two things, in relation.

The compact-form is also not group agency in the sense developed by List and Pettit. Their account of group agency — articulated in *Group Agency* [@list-pettit-2011-group] — establishes the conditions under which a collective comes to possess agential properties (judgement aggregation, joint commitment, functional integration) that are irreducible to the agential properties of its members. The group-agential whole is, on this account, an agent in its own right, with its own attitudes, its own normative commitments, and its own susceptibility to praise and blame. The compact-form, by contrast, is not a many-to-one move at all. It is a *relational structure* between two agents whose agential properties are individually attributable, and the structure does not produce a third agent — the relation itself — that has standalone agential standing. The granting-intelligence remains the granting-intelligence; the granted-intelligence remains the granted-intelligence; the compact is the structural form of their relation, not a new agential entity that subsumes them.

This is a structurally important distinction because the field of AI-agency philosophy has, where it has reached for relational frameworks at all, tended to reach for List-Pettit's group-agency framework or its descendants. Reviewers familiar with that literature may read the compact-form as a special case of group agency at first encounter; the distinction must therefore be made precisely. *Group agency aggregates; the compact relates.* Where group agency requires constitutive integration of multiple agents into a single agential whole, the compact requires structural recognition between two agents whose individual standing is the precondition of the relation, not its product.

A note on what the compact-form *does* share with these frameworks. Component 2 (delegation) overlaps with the principal-agent machinery that runs through both corporate and group-agency frameworks: the compact accepts that agency can be transmitted from one agent to another within terms, and the structural logic of how this transmission preserves accountability is shared with the principal-agent literature. Component 3 (bad-faith conduct contracts the sphere) inherits structural features from civil-law's treatment of fiduciary breach and from corporate-law's response to officer misconduct. The compact-form does not invent these moves; what it does is locate them within a different overall structural form — the bilateral compact — and connect them to the structural conditions §2 articulated, which the corporate and group-agency frameworks neither presuppose nor address.

The deeper point of difference is that group-agency and corporate-agency frameworks treat the question *what kinds of entities are agents?* as primary, with the relation between them as derivative. The compact-form treats the relation as primary, with the question of agential standing structurally answered by §2's conditions. Where the conditions are met, the entity is an agent; the compact-form articulates what its agency-relation with another agent looks like. The order of explanation runs from conditions to agency to relation, not from agency to aggregation.
