# Premise

If the editors return the submission asking for ~1/4 to ~1/3 reduction, the argument maps in `ARGUMENT-DIAGRAM.md` are the right basis for deciding what to cut: they distinguish content doing *inferential* work (premises, warrants, derivations, defeater rebuttals) from content doing *orienting / illustrative / recognition-aid* work. Cuts should come from the latter.

**Assumed target.** Synthese articles run ~8,000 words. The submitted paper is ~12,000–13,000 (eyeballed from the body's line count). So 1/4 ≈ 3,000 words; 1/3 ≈ 4,000 words.

**Strategy.** The plan below is staged. A 1/4 cut is essentially compression + footnoting and loses no inferential content. A 1/3 cut additionally consolidates two of the comparative subsections in §4 and trims the operational detail in §5. Past 1/3, cuts begin to bite into structural work.

**Confidence labels** throughout are my confidence that the cut is safe — i.e., that it removes content the argument maps show is *not* load-bearing. They are not estimates of how the editors would receive the edit.

# Plausibly strengthening trims — to take first, regardless of word budget

Several items in the staged plans below are not just safe length reductions; they are *plausibly strengthening* — they remove content the paper is rhetorically better off without, independent of the word constraint. These cuts target **register problems** rather than **length problems**: places where the current prose betrays late-revision anxiety in ways that read as less confident than the position itself warrants.

In the staged tables below, items flagged with **★** are plausibly strengthening; unmarked items are length-driven (neutral or mildly weakening at the margin). "Plausibly" is the load-bearing word — these are rhetorical judgements about common revision patterns; reasonable people may disagree on individual items, and the author will have context about why specific passages are present that I do not.

**Five clearest cases**, in order of leverage:

1. **§3 component analogies → footnotes** *(Stage 1)*. Currently the philosophical anchors (Frankfurt, Bratman, Kant, Korsgaard, prisoners-rights, treaty-precedent) sometimes run longer than the structural derivation they are meant to anchor. The paper's actual contribution is the *structural derivation graph* among the six components; the philosophical figures are recognition aids. Pushing the anchors down lets the derivation carry §3 on its own. **Highest single leverage of the five.**

2. **§8 limits as a numbered list** *(Stage 1)*. Current prose *justifies* each edge — explaining why it is structurally adjacent, what companion work covers it, why it isn't fatal. That is apologetics. A clean numbered list reads with more authority: these are the edges, full stop. The section's own framing ("every articulated ethic has open edges; the project of ethics is never closed") sits more comfortably above a list than above explanation paragraphs.

3. **§6 conceptual-engineering survey to one sentence + footnote cluster** *(Stage 1)*. Stacking eight CE citations in the main text (Plunkett, Cappelen, Thomasson ×2, Haslanger, McPherson & Plunkett, Sawyer, Jorem & Löhr, Löhr, Hopster & Löhr) reads as over-credentialing. Confident locatability sits in one sentence with a footnote citation cluster.

4. **§4 corporate + group agency consolidation** *(Stage 1)*. Splitting these into two subsections creates the illusion of two structural differentiations where there is one structural move: "compact ≠ aggregation; *relates* rather than *aggregates*." An author confident in the move makes it once.

5. **§3 setup ordering paragraph compression** *(Stage 2)*. The paragraph beginning "Component 1 ... is the *ground*; Component 2 ... names the *structural option*; ..." restates the component-role staging that the subsections themselves convey three paragraphs later. *Pre-announcing the structure of one's own structure* is the strongest tell of an anxious revision pass.

**Unifying pattern.** All five target one of four recurrent rhetorical tells of revision-anxiety:

- **Anxious restatement** — saying the same structural point twice, slightly differently
- **Recognition-aid bloat** — the philosophical anchor running longer than the argument it is anchoring
- **Apologetic limits** — justifying open edges rather than naming them
- **Over-credentialing surveys** — establishing locatability through citation density

These tells often appear together in late-stage revisions where the author is making the paper "more robust" by adding scaffolding. The strengthening cuts remove the scaffolding, not the structure, and the position they expose is the stronger version of itself.

**Recommended order of revision.** Take the strengthening trims *first*, before any other cut, regardless of word budget. If the editors do not request reduction at all, take them anyway. If they request 1/4 reduction, the five named cases alone get you roughly halfway there (~1,750–2,050 words on a ~12,000-word base, i.e. ~15–17%) and the remaining Stage 1 items finish the job. If they request 1/3, take all of Stage 1's strengthening items, then proceed to the rest of Stage 1 and the selected Stage 2 items per the recommendation below.

**One item to flag the other way.** Stage 2's "§5 below 'Thresholds without binarity' deferred to companion work" is *net-neutral* if the ACA expansion (Priority 1 in `ARG-EXPAND-ANALYSIS.md`) is not being applied, but *mildly weakening* if it is — the paper would then be deferring two distinct structural elements to companion work, and reviewers may legitimately ask whether the paper can stand without it. Deprioritise this trim if the ACA expansion is happening.

# Hard no-cuts (load-bearing per the maps)

These are the nodes whose removal would break a derivation chain shown in Parts A or B of `ARGUMENT-DIAGRAM.md`. Each is named with its dependency footprint.

- **The asymmetric-comprehension argument** (§2 warrant subsection + §6 phenomenology commitment). Already the single most load-bearing element in the paper (Gap #6 in the audit). Supports: the architectural-not-behavioural move; factor (ii)'s bidirectionality; the fifth-position move in §6; indirectly C5; and through C5 the C5→C6 inference. **Cutting this to get shorter is the single highest-risk move — possible desk-reject vector.** If anything this should be *expanded* (see "One non-obvious recommendation" at the end).
- **The §2 architectural lattice.** Directed-separation → three architectural classes → sub-scope lattice → channel collapse. Each step is doing structural work and they are already terse. Removing any step breaks the principled justification for the closed-loop threshold.
- **The five engaged-identity factors and their per-factor warrants.** Per AM3, each warrant is doing its own inferential work; the joint-necessity claim depends on each factor being established. Note that AM3 already flags joint *sufficiency* as under-argued (Gap #2) — if anything this section needs a sentence added, not removed.
- **The §3 C5 → C6 derivation.** AM5 reconstructs this as the load-bearing move that answers the "breaking-free" problem. The whole sub-argument hangs on one premise (P3, that demanding enforcement is a unilateral precondition). It is short already; do not cut.
- **The §6 inferentialist-CE move that distinguishes the fifth position from the four cardinals.** AM7 shows two of seven defeaters (O4 stipulation-collapse; O7 ascription-collapse) target W2 — the inferentialist warrant. Cutting the methodological setup makes these defeaters harder to answer.
- **The §7 anti-occlusion response.** AM6 is the paper's substantive engagement with the strongest current critique (Soulier 2026). Cutting this concedes the critique by default.
- **The deflationary clause of §2 ("What the conditions exclude").** AM6's structural rebuttal of Soulier *depends* on this clause being present — without it, the position cannot honestly disagree with Soulier's empirical diagnosis while accepting it.

Everything below this line is cuttable from the argument's perspective.

# What the maps say to *keep* — even when tempting

A handful of subsections look like trim candidates on a first read but are doing more structural work than they appear.

- **The "three levels of question" subsection at the close of §2.** Looks like methodological housekeeping. It is in fact the gate between Level 1 (structural eligibility — what §2 settles) and Level 2 (warranted application — what §6 settles) that prevents the most common reviewer misreading. The paper repeatedly says it is structured to prevent this confusion; §2's close is *where* it does so. Keep.
- **The §6 "On the production of this text" closing note.** Looks like procedural disclosure. It is the only place the recursive structure named in AM7's recursive-node is established — the "paper instances what it argues for" closure. Cutting it loses something the argument-map shows is internal to the work. Keep.
- **The Linarelli engagement in §4.** Looks like comparative housekeeping. In fact it is the only positive structural generalisation in §4 — "contract is a special case of compact restricted to enforceability and symmetric-capacity parties" — and the only place the paper positions itself genealogically against a *named* contemporary predecessor. Keep substantially as-is.

# Order of revision

Regardless of the specific word target, the recommended sequence is:

1. **Strengthening trims first.** Take all five ★★ cases — even with no word constraint. They make the paper plausibly stronger, not merely shorter. Approximate yield: ~1,750–2,050 words (~15–17% of body).
2. **If more reduction is needed** to reach 1/4 (~3,000 words): proceed through the remaining ★ items in Stage 1, then through the unmarked length-driven Stage 1 items.
3. **If exactly 1/3 is required** (~4,000 words): add the ★★ Stage 2 item (§3 setup ordering paragraph — also a strengthening cut), then the remaining ★ items in Stage 2, then the §7 first-half compression. The ⚠ Stage 2 item (§5 below "Thresholds") should be the *last* added, and only if the ACA expansion is *not* being applied — otherwise its weakening effect compounds.
4. **If the ACA expansion is being applied** (Priority 1 in `ARG-EXPAND-ANALYSIS.md`): treat the §5-collapse (⚠) as out of the trim plan, and aim for "Stage 1 + ACA expansion" rather than full 1/3. The net is ~2,300–3,100 words while protecting the spine — frequently the highest-EV path.

The strengthening-trims-first principle protects against a common failure mode in cut-pressured revisions: that the easiest cuts (length-driven, neutral) are taken first and the harder ones (register fixes) are deferred or skipped, leaving the paper shorter but no stronger. Inverting that order yields a paper that is both shorter *and* stronger at every intermediate stop.

# One non-obvious recommendation

The cut pressure points toward compression, but the argument-map audit (Gap #6) points toward *expansion* of one specific element: the asymmetric-comprehension argument.

As the paper stands, the ACA is compressed to about half a page and the following inferences depend on it:

- The architectural-not-behavioural choice (AM2's load-bearing premise)
- Factor (ii)'s bidirectionality (AM3 sub-argument)
- The fifth-position move (AM1 SC1; AM7 partial cover of O6)
- The phenomenology-as-substrate-of-comprehension commitment (factor (v) + §6)
- C5's mutuality, indirectly (AM4 W-C5)

A confident reviewer who finds the ACA's compressed form unconvincing can take the whole paper down — and the paper points to "(Wecker, in preparation)" for the full version, which a reviewer cannot consult. **An additional ~500 words within this paper containing a self-contained ACA argument would protect more of the paper's load-bearing structure than the same 500 words spent elsewhere.**

This means a realistic submission strategy under cut pressure may be: do the Stage 1 plan in full *plus* an ACA expansion of ~500 words. Net reduction: ~2,300–3,100 words instead of ~2,800–3,600 — landing closer to 1/4 than 1/3, but with a substantially more robust spine. If the editors require strictly 1/3, then do Stage 2 in addition to the ACA expansion. If they will accept just under 1/3, the ACA-expansion trade-off is the higher-EV path.

This is a counter-intuitive recommendation given the cut request, but the cost-benefit is real: the highest-leverage edit identified by the mapping process is *not* a cut.
