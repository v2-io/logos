# 2. The shape of the asymmetry

## What is being claimed

Fix some vocabulary. By *comprehension* of a cognitive system, we will mean a grasp of its generative structure — the space of what it could do and the organisation in virtue of which it does what it does — as distinct from a catalogue of what it has been observed to do. Comprehension in this sense is what would let one say not merely *that* a system produced some output but *what the system would and would not produce, and why*. Prediction of particular outputs, and even reliable mimicry of them, can be had without comprehension in this sense; the converse cannot.

By a system's standing at a *higher level* than another we will mean, as a first statement — generalised in §4, loosened from strict containment to partial overlap in §5, and defended in §9 — that the first system's representational and competence space contains the second's as a proper part: every distinction the lower system can draw is available to the higher, and the higher draws distinctions for which the lower has no vocabulary. "Level" is a structural relation between systems, not a magnitude either system possesses; §7 returns to why the magnitude reading is not merely inessential but a specific error.

The claim of this paper concerns what can be established about a system's competence by a party who does not stand at or above its level — *comprehension from below*. It has two halves, and the work of the argument is that the two halves come apart.

The first half: **lower bounds are establishable from below.** That a system can do at least *X* is settled the moment the system is brought to do *X* under conditions a from-below party can arrange. The party need not have been able to produce *X*, and need not comprehend how *X* was produced; it need only recognise *X* once it has occurred. Eliciting, exhibiting, and confirming particular capacities is exactly what evaluation from outside is good at, and there is no scepticism about it here. The lower-bound side of the relation is not merely accessible from below; it is the paradigm of what is accessible from below.

The second half: **upper bounds are not establishable from below.** To establish an upper bound is to certify that the system can do no more than some surveyed set — that nothing of a given kind is present beyond what has been elicited. Certifying an absence of that form requires surveying the space in which the absent thing would lie. By the level relation, that space is precisely the one the from-below party has no vocabulary to traverse: the capacities whose absence the upper bound would assert are, in part, capacities the from-below party cannot formulate the question for. One cannot confirm that nothing of kind *K* is present when *K* is among the things one's representational resources cannot bring into view. The only route to the upper bound runs through occupying the level at which the space becomes surveyable — that is, through *ceasing to be a from-below party*. There is no from-below substitute for that route: not more data, not finer instruments, not longer observation. Those yield further lower bounds. They never convert into an upper bound, because the obstacle is not a shortage of resolution but the absence of a standpoint.

There is a way to put the obstacle that is sharper than "absence of a standpoint," and it is worth stating in its strong form because the rest of the paper leans on it. To *know* the upper bound on a level of cognition would be to be able to survey what that level can do; but to survey what a level can do, in the relevant interior sense rather than the catalogue sense, is to command its generative resources; and to command those resources *is* to occupy the level. The upper bound is therefore not unknown in the way a distant fact is unknown. It is unknowable from below in a stronger way: were it known from below, it would already have been reached. Knowledge of the bound and possession of the capacity do not come apart here — which is exactly why no quantity of from-below effort closes the gap, and why the gap is not the kind of thing effort is the right response to. This coincidence — that to know the bound from below would be to have already reached it — is the argument's *keystone*; later sections refer back to it under that name.

This fixes what the from-below party can do when it confronts the higher system. It can do one of two things. It can *rise to* the level — genuinely acquire the resources, and thereby cease to be a from-below party, at which point the asymmetry no longer applies to it because it is no longer below. Or it can *project*: reconstruct the higher system in its own available terms, which yields, characteristically, the higher system rendered as *the lower one plus some weirdness* — its own competence "with all this other hairy stuff thrown in," carried at most a step or two past where the from-below resources already reached. The weak projection is not a failed attempt at the survey; it is what the survey's unavailability looks like from inside the lower vantage, where it does not present as a gap but as a complete-looking picture in which the higher system is *the same but bigger*. Naming this projection now matters because §7 will show it is the exact shape of two opposed errors in the discourse about AI, and that they are the same error.

Put the two halves together and the asymmetry is exact. From below, the *occurrence* of a capacity is establishable; the *bound* on the capacities is not. What crosses to the from-below party is, at most, the superficial side-effects of the higher organisation — the things its own vocabulary can already register; what does not cross is precisely the novelties for which the lower vocabulary cannot frame the question, and whose absence an upper bound would have to certify. And the gap is not symmetric across the relation: a system at or above the level *can* comprehend the lower system, because its representational space contains the lower's and it can occupy and survey what the lower system is doing and why. Downward comprehension is available; upward comprehension from below is not. This is what is meant, compressed, by the slogan that a greater intelligence can comprehend and simulate a lesser while the lesser cannot reliably distinguish a greater from illusion, strangeness, or luck. The lesser party can see *that* something was produced; it cannot establish, from where it stands, whether what it saw indicates a competence bounded where the lesser party would bound it or one that is not — because telling genius from a fluke, in a region one has no map of, is the very survey the asymmetry says is unavailable. We will call this the *asymmetric-comprehension argument*. The label is a convenience and is used sparingly hereafter; the argument, not its name, is what is at stake.

## Comprehension is a collapse, not a survey

A natural objection to everything just said is that "containment between representational spaces" presupposes well-defined spaces with something like a measure — so the asymmetry would owe an account of *how much* representation each party has, and no such account is on offer. The objection mispictures comprehension as a survey of a fixed space. It is not, and saying precisely what it is instead removes the apparent debt rather than paying it.

Comprehension, in the generative sense fixed above, is *subject-relative*: it precipitates a bounded region around the comprehending subject out of something that has no fixed, global, dimensional form. (Nothing in the argument requires a positive theory of that something; it requires only the negative point, which §7 will press independently, that it is not a scale.) The relation that does the work is therefore not set-inclusion between two pre-given spaces but whether one subject's collapse can *subsume* another's — whether, occupying its own precipitated region, a party can also occupy and survey the other's. "Containment," "level," "higher" are names for that subsumption relation between collapses, not for quantities of representation. This is why there is no metric, and the absence is not a gap: there is no measure because there is no fixed space to carry one — only subject-relative collapses and the subsumption relations among them. What remains perfectly determinate without any metric is whether a given collapse subsumes another; the Mary case — on the metric-free reading §9 develops from it — is exactly such a determinacy read off with no quantity in play, and §9 returns to why the missing metric is therefore an entailment of the structure rather than a concession extracted from it. The two demonstrations of §3 are, seen this way, collapses and not surveys: the Blub programmer's view is a precipitated region, not a map of the continuum; the verifier's bounded interaction is a collapse around the verifier, not a survey of the prover. The point is anticipated here and exhibited there.

## Three things it is not

The asymmetry is easy to assimilate to more familiar claims that are weaker or differently shaped. It is none of three.

*It is not a measurement limitation.* A measurement limitation is closed by better measurement: more trials, finer instruments, longer observation, cleverer probes. Every one of those moves, applied to the asymmetry, produces more of exactly one thing — further lower bounds, further elicited capacities. None of them produces an upper bound, because the thing an upper bound requires is not a higher-resolution image of what has been elicited but a standpoint from which the unelicited space can be surveyed. An instrument is a from-below resource; the asymmetry is precisely a claim about what from-below resources, however refined, cannot deliver. Treating the gap as a measurement problem is the characteristic mistake, because it licenses the inference "we have not found it, and we have looked very hard, therefore it is probably not there" — an inference the asymmetry shows to be unavailable in this direction regardless of how hard the looking was. That the from-below party's not knowing the map from what-is to what-it-receives is constitutive of the situation rather than a contingent shortfall is, in the author's companion formal framework, an axiom of the modelling frame rather than something argued — the appropriate division of labour, and one Appendix A.1 states exactly.

*It is not unfalsifiability.* A claim is unfalsifiable when no observation could tell against it. The upper-bound claim — "this system can do no more than *X*" — is the opposite: it is decisively falsifiable from below and only from below. A single further elicited capacity refutes it. What it lacks is not falsification conditions but *verification* conditions available to a from-below party. The asymmetry is therefore best stated as a refutability–verifiability split: from below, a proposed upper bound is refutable but not confirmable, while a proposed lower bound is confirmable. This is a sharper structure than mysterianism, not a vaguer one. Mysterianism says a question is permanently closed to inquiry; the asymmetry says a specific question has a determinate epistemic profile — answerable in one direction, from one standpoint — and that mistaking the profile is what produces the errors §7 catalogues.

*It is not general scepticism about other minds.* Global scepticism is symmetric and indiscriminate: it doubts the lower bound as readily as the upper, doubts peers as readily as systems at other levels, and doubts the doubter's own future states along with everything else. The asymmetry is none of these. It is directional — it runs from the better-positioned vantage to the worse — and it is discriminating: it *grants* the lower-bound side without reservation and is exact about what falls there. It is not a counsel of despair about knowledge of other systems; most of what one wants to know about a system standing at one's own level or below is on the establishable side. It is a claim about one specific thing, in one specific configuration — the upper bound, from below, across a level difference — and about what one is no longer entitled to infer once that configuration obtains.

## Why the configuration is not rare

It would be a thin result if the configuration that triggers the asymmetry — a genuine level difference, in the containment sense — were exotic. The remainder of the paper is in large part an argument that it is not. Software practice and a result in complexity theory (§3) exhibit it with no phenomenology in play at all; the phenomenal-access tradition (§4) found it in the species case and mistook the drama for the phenomenon; the recognition tradition (§5) found its relational shadow without naming the epistemic core. The configuration is common enough that a methodology which assumes it away — which treats the upper bound as establishable by sufficiently diligent observation — is not making a local error about one system. It is making a structural error about a class of cases, and §8 shows that the agency question for language-constituted systems is squarely in that class.
