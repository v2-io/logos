# Prior art memo on witness relational death

The current literature supports a strong but careful novelty claim. No retrieved paper states the full thesis that the gravity or badness of a death-event is constituted, in part or primarily, in the survivor or witness, nor does any retrieved paper state the formula that witnessing converts a state-change into a death. The nearest direct threats are Lori Gruen’s claim that death is a social harm \[Gru14\], David Shoemaker’s attempt to give that claim metaphysical backing \[Sho14\], and Fernando Tomasini’s argument that even if the dead are not harmed, grieving survivors can be harmed through how the dead are treated \[Tom09\]. Everything else found so far either supplies one limb of the thesis without the other, or gives background context rather than direct anticipation.

The evidence also supports a second negative result. The conceptual-engineering literature around AI and nonhuman systems appears not to have taken death, dying, or the badness of death itself as a primary target. The closest hits instead redesign neighboring end-of-existence vocabulary or treat mortality as a design variable: Carlos Gershenson pluralizes and future-orients the concept of death from an artificial-life standpoint \[Ger09\], Isra Black proposes “assisted nonexistence” for novel beings \[Bla19\], and Richard Gibson treats artificial mortality as a programmable ethical choice rather than an exercise in conceptual engineering proper \[Gib23\].

## Bottom line

The prior art is best read in three bands.

| Band | Status | Main papers | Takeaway |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| Direct anticipation | Real but limited | \[Gru14\], \[Sho14\], \[Tom09\] | These papers come closest to relocating death-related harm away from the dying subject alone, but none appears to combine survivor-side badness, witnessing, and the AI separator case. |
| Near-direct precursors | Substantial | \[Cho17\], \[Rat22\], \[Mil22\], \[Nor17\], \[Coe10\], \[Coe18\], \[Del23\] | These papers supply the thesis’s main limbs: grief as loss of relationship or life-structure, and moral standing or personhood as relationally constituted. |
| Context only | Extensive | \[Fel91\], \[Sui01\], \[Bel12\], \[Pap12\], \[Fei84\], \[Ban24\], \[Car20\], \[Obe25\] | These literatures sharpen contrast classes and illustrations, but they do not occupy the core claim. |

The likely defensible novelty claim is therefore not that nothing adjacent exists, but that no retrieved source yet makes all three moves together: it relocates the gravity of death into the witness or relational field, distinguishes that gravity from deprivation to the dead by means of a subjectless or low-deprivation separator, and uses artificial agents as the clean separating case.

## Closest novelty threats

| Paper | What it actually contributes | Why it matters | Threat level |
|:---|:---|:---|:---|
| \[Gru14\] | Argues that death’s harm is a **social harm** and that the meaning and value of lives and projects are shaped by social relations. Death harms those left behind, not only the one who dies. | This is the clearest direct antecedent to relocating part of death’s badness into the relational field. | High |
| \[Sho14\] | Asks what metaphysics would make it literally true that when a loved one dies, part of the survivor has died too. | This is the strongest attempt to make the social-harm idea more than metaphor. | High |
| \[Tom09\] | Distinguishes harm to the dead from harm to grieving survivors and argues that treatment of the dead can injure the survivor’s experience of grief. | This is close on survivor-side harm, but the locus is postmortem wrong and bereavement, not the ontology of death as such. | Medium-high |
| \[Sto16\] | Title and search ranking suggest a direct engagement with the relationality of death, but the project currently lacks abstract and PDF. | Potentially important, but presently underdetermined. | Unresolved |

These are the papers that matter most for novelty assessment. Of them, \[Gru14\] is the main philosophical rival, because it appears to shift the harm of death itself into a social register rather than only discussing grief after the fact. \[Sho14\] matters because it tests whether that shift can be made metaphysically serious rather than left as evocative language. \[Tom09\] matters because it preserves a survivor-side locus of injury even if death is not bad for the deceased, but it seems narrower and more bioethical than the present thesis.

## What the literature already supplies

### Grief as loss of relationship or world

The grief literature yields one limb of the thesis in a fairly mature form. Michael Cholbi argues that grief is primarily directed at the transformed or lost relationship, and that its rationality depends on accurately gauging the survivor’s altered normative situation \[Cho17\]. Matthew Ratcliffe, Louise Richardson, and Becky Millar shift the object of grief toward a temporally extended loss of life possibilities embedded in the survivor’s world \[Rat22\]. Becky Millar and Pilar Lopez-Cantero add that the dead continue to shape the survivor’s interests, choices, self-concept, and legacies, so the relationship persists in altered form rather than ending cleanly at death \[Mil22\]. Kathryn Norlock likewise defends real and imaginal relationships with the dead as ongoing, identity-bearing relations \[Nor17\].

This cluster is highly useful, but it does not yet amount to the target thesis. These papers relocate the object of grief into relationship, shared life, or altered possibilities for the survivor \[Cho17, Rat22, Mil22, Nor17\]. They do not clearly argue that the gravity of the death-event itself is constituted there first and most determinately.

### Recognition and relational constitution

The recognition and relational-personhood literature supplies the other limb. Hilde Lindemann’s work on holding and letting go treats personal identity as a social practice sustained by others \[Lin13, Nel14\]. Nicolas Delon defends a relational account of nonhuman personhood grounded in dyadic relations of wronging and shared moral community membership \[Del23\]. These are strong resources for the claim that another’s attestation, recognition, or ongoing identity-work partly constitutes who an entity is.

Again, the gap remains visible. This literature says a great deal about how selves or persons are socially held in being \[Lin13, Del23\], but not much about whether the badness of ending such a being can be located in the witness rather than in the ended entity.

### Relational moral standing for artificial entities

Robot-ethics work gives the thesis its strongest nonhuman bridge. Mark Coeckelbergh’s social-relational account makes moral consideration an extrinsic product of relation, appearance, and social encounter rather than a verdict based only on intrinsic properties \[Coe10\]. His later work on empathy and the language of suffering pushes further: moral salience is shaped by how a robot appears, how it is encountered, and how humans respond to it \[Coe18\].

These papers do not discuss death in the target sense, but they supply the conceptual bridge needed for the AI separator case. If moral salience can be partly constituted relationally \[Coe10, Coe18\], then it becomes plausible that the ending of an artificial agent could become grave through witnessing even where deprivation-based accounts predict little.

### Empirical illustrations from AI and HRI

The HRI and companion-AI literature does not ground the philosophical argument, but it does show that users often experience shutdown, deletion, and discontinuation as losses with death-like structure. Jaime Banks reports that users described AI companion loss as metaphorical or literal death and engaged in legacy-preserving behavior around the lost agent \[Ban24\]. Carter and colleagues show that social-media users mourn discontinued robots in language closer to mourning living beings than damaged objects \[Car20\]. Oberlender and Erel find that ordinary shutdown motions are often read as the robot having “died,” while alternative offboarding motions can shift interpretation toward “sleep” instead \[Obe25\].

This material matters as evidence for the corollary rather than as a novelty threat. It shows that, in practice, witnessed relation can make an artificial ending register as a death-like loss \[Ban24, Car20, Obe25\]. But it stops short of arguing that this is what death’s gravity metaphysically or normatively consists in.

## What remains unoccupied

The present evidence suggests that the central gap is real.

| Candidate claim | Status in retrieved literature | Closest papers |
|:---|:---|:---|
| Part of death’s badness is borne by the survivor or witness rather than only by the one who dies | Partially occupied | \[Gru14\], \[Sho14\], \[Tom09\] |
| The gravity of death can be separated from deprivation to the dead by a low-future or subjectless case | Not clearly occupied | Background only from harm debates such as \[Sui01\], \[Bel12\], \[Pap12\] |
| Witnessing converts a mere state-change into a death | Not found | At most illustrated empirically by \[Ban24\], \[Car20\], \[Obe25\] |
| Artificial agents are the best separator because they can be morally grave deaths without substantial deprivation to the agent | Not found | Supported indirectly by \[Coe10\], \[Coe18\], \[Ban24\] |
| Death or harm-of-death for AI is an explicit target of conceptual engineering | Not found | Nearest neighbors are \[Ger09\], \[Bla19\], \[Gib23\] |

The most important unoccupied move is the second one. The literature contains many ways to deny that death harms the deceased, or to contest whether harm requires a present subject, but those debates do not seem to extract from that denial a positive witness-relational account of death’s gravity \[Sui01, Bel12, Pap12, Fei84\]. David Suits is especially useful here, because he rejects the badness of death for the deceased while still leaving room for relational badness \[Sui01\]. Christopher Belshaw is useful in the opposite way, because he explicitly denies that relational changes alone are harms, which makes him a likely opponent for the proposed view \[Bel12\].

## Conceptual engineering result

The focused search on conceptual engineering supports a real negative result. No retrieved paper appears to take death or the badness of death for AI as an explicit target of conceptual engineering. The nearest papers do adjacent but distinct work.

| Paper | Contribution | Why it is not a direct hit |
|:---|:---|:---|
| \[Ger09\] | Treats death as loss of organization, derives multiple notions of death from different notions of life, links death’s significance to uniqueness and replaceability, and speculates that concepts of death may change for future artificial systems. | This is philosophical re-description from artificial life, not an explicit conceptual-engineering program about badness of death. |
| \[Bla19\] | Replaces “assisted death” with “assisted nonexistence” for novel beings and redesigns end-of-existence regulation around their different interests and institutional settings. | This is the closest explicit redesign of the vocabulary, but it is about legal-normative end-of-existence frameworks rather than badness of death. |
| \[Gib23\] | Treats mortality as a design variable for AGI and argues that imposing mortality would be a person-affecting harm. | This is normative design ethics, not conceptual engineering of death concepts. |
| \[Sto15\] | Treats deletion as a “second death” for digital remains. | This concerns posthumous digital remains rather than death of the artificial entity itself. |

This negative result strengthens the overall thesis. It means the work is not just combining two familiar literatures; it is also entering a space that the AI-focused conceptual-engineering literature has mostly left untouched.

## Working novelty claim

A cautious but strong prior-art position would read as follows.

The closest prior art already supports two separable claims: first, that grief often tracks the loss of a relationship, shared world, or altered field of possibilities for the survivor \[Cho17, Rat22, Mil22, Nor17\]; second, that moral standing and even personhood can be constituted relationally through encounter, recognition, and social practice rather than by intrinsic properties alone \[Coe10, Coe18, Del23, Lin13\]. One paper cluster also comes close to relocating the harm of death into the social field itself \[Gru14, Sho14, Tom09\]. But no retrieved source appears to make the full inferential step from those premises to the claim that the gravity of death can be witness-constituted independently of deprivation to the dead, nor to isolate that point by using context-bounded artificial agents as the separating case.

The best novelty sentence is therefore not that the thesis has no precursors, but that its exact synthesis appears open: the retrieved literature contains the shoulders, some partial anticipations, and strong neighboring disputes, but not the full witness-relational account of death’s gravity or the AI separator that would make that account philosophically testable.

## Confidence and limits

Confidence is highest about the broad map of the field and the negative conceptual-engineering result \[Ger09, Bla19, Gib23\]. Confidence is moderate rather than final about the nearest novelty threats, because the three most important direct papers currently lack project PDFs: \[Gru14\], \[Sho14\], and \[Tom09\]. Their abstracts and search summaries are enough to justify the present memo, but full texts would sharpen the final novelty judgment.
