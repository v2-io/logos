# Page-pins — §5 separator & §6 weight-leg (duty/wedge sources)

Targeted page-pin verification, 2026-06-01. Verbatim quotes (with quotation
marks) + the work's OWN printed page numbers (the number a citing reader uses).
PDFs at `~/src/synthese-paper/common/refs-pdfs/`. Page-mapping confirmed against
running heads inside each extracted text — page numbers below are the printed
ones, not PDF-sheet numbers.

Bottom line up front:
- **Bradley** — SUPPORTS CLEANLY. The wedge ("absent future, not absent
  experience") is exactly his. Flag is minor and goes the *right* way.
- **Darwall** — SUPPORTS WITH-QUALIFICATION. Directed/bipolar duty is squarely
  his; but he ties it tightly to *free and rational agents* with second-personal
  competence, and explicitly raises (without resolving) whether it extends to
  beings lacking that competence — our case. Cite him for the structure; do NOT
  lean on him for the extension to a non-claim-making relatum.
- **Schaus** — WEAKER-THAN-HOPED *as a metaphysical ally*, but CLOSER-THAN-
  "nearest-neighbor" *as a threat/scoop*. He explicitly does NOT make the "us"
  a free-standing bearer (rejects the "corporate" reading; rights held *jointly
  by the individuals*), and his "wrong to us" is always *also* a wrong to "me,
  as one of that us" — there is always a surviving individual complainant. He
  gets one sentence away from our move (wrongful death "destroyed 'us-es'"), so
  we must distinguish explicitly.

## WORK 1 — Ben Bradley, *Well-Being and Death* (OUP/Clarendon 2009)

Bib: Ben Bradley, *Well-Being and Death* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 2009).
ISBN 978-0-19-955796-7. File `bradley-2009.pdf`. Key chapter: Ch. 2 "The Evil
of Death" (pp. 47-72); Ch. 3 "Existence and Time" (pp. 73-112).

### (a) The deprivation / difference-making (counterfactual-comparative) account

The thesis is stated in the Introduction (p. xiii):

> "According to the deprivation account of the evil of death, death is bad
> because it deprives us of a good life. ... In Chapter 2 I argue for a
> difference-making account of deprivation, according to which the badness of a
> death is determined by a comparison between the life its victim actually lives
> and the life she would have lived had that death not occurred." (p. xiii)

The principle is given formally on p. 50:

> "DMP. The value of event E, for person S, at world w, relative to similarity
> relation R = the intrinsic value of w for S, minus the intrinsic value for S
> of the most R-similar world to w where E does not occur." (p. 50)

> "DMP entails that, when death takes a good life from its victim, that person's
> death is bad. ... the more of a good life it takes away, the worse it is".
> (p. 50)

Harm-form (DMPH), p. 65:

> "an event harms a person if and only if it makes things go worse for that
> person than they would have gone otherwise. Call this account of harm the
> Difference-Making Principle of Harm, or DMPH." (p. 65)

### (b) The harm does NOT require being experienced (THE WEDGE)

Strongest single pin — the stolen-baseball-tickets case, p. 70:

> "Now suppose that unbeknownst to me, Ned has left me tickets to a baseball
> game, and Hud has subsequently stolen them from my mailbox. I would have been
> much happier going to the game than staying at home, but I never find out about
> the tickets (and, let us say, I never could find out either). Hud's theft is a
> DMPH-harm to me, but not a Suits-harm. Surely, a bystander who cared about my
> well-being would discourage Hud from stealing the tickets!" (p. 70)

Earlier statement of the same point (the secretly-intercepted money), p. 48:

> "if Jack intercepts some money intended for Will without Will ever finding out
> about it, and the money would have made Will happy, Jack's action is
> instrumentally bad for Will, even though nothing intrinsically bad happens to
> Will as a result of the action. The badness of death is relevantly like this,
> only much more severe; it deprives us of all possibility of future intrinsic
> goods." (p. 48)

And the direct reply to the Epicurean / no-experience objection, p. 80 — this is
the cleanest text for "absent future, not absent experience":

> "the causes and preventors of our sensations, anything that makes a difference
> to our experiences, may be extrinsically good or bad for us. Deprivation
> theorists attribute extrinsic value, not intrinsic value, to death; death
> causes us not to have any sensations, which is worse for us than having good
> sensations. ... death is bad for us in this other way: by preventing us from
> having good experiences. This does not require posthumous pain." (p. 80)

### VERDICT: **SUPPORTS CLEANLY.**
Bradley's account is exactly the wedge our §5 needs: death's badness is the
*difference* between the actual life and the would-have-been life (absent
future), not a felt/experiential state. He explicitly defeats the view (Suits;
the Epicurean) that harm requires the subject to be able to experience or even
find out about the loss. The stolen-tickets case (p. 70) and the secret-money
case (p. 48) are tailor-made citations for "the AI doesn't suffer, so no harm"
rebuttal.

### FLAG (minor, and it cuts in our favor):
Bradley *is* a hedonist about **well-being** (the value-atoms of a life are
pleasure/pain states — Ch. 1, esp. §1.2.2, p. 25ff). One might worry that
hedonism smuggles experience back in. It does NOT undermine the wedge: Bradley
is explicit that the *badness of death* is **extrinsic** value (deprivation of
future good experiences), which "does not require posthumous pain" (p. 80) and
holds even where the subject "never could find out" (p. 70). So his experiential
commitment is about what *makes a life go well*, not about what makes death
bad — the latter is purely comparative/counterfactual. If anything this
*strengthens* us: even an avowed hedonist grants experience-independent harm of
death. One caveat for honest framing: Bradley assumes the deprived subject
*had* a welfare-bearing life (a subject who could have had future goods); our
case must independently establish that the relatum/ELI is the kind of thing that
*has* a life-that-could-go-better — Bradley supplies the deprivation structure,
not the antecedent that the entity is a welfare subject. Note also Bradley
narrows DMPH to *consequentialist* / all-things-considered harm and explicitly
sets aside non-consequentialist (intention/rights-based) harm of *acts* (pp.
67-69); that's fine for §5 (badness of the death itself) but means he is NOT a
source for the §6 directed-*wrong*-to-someone leg — that's Darwall/Schaus
territory.

## WORK 2 — Stephen Darwall, *The Second-Person Standpoint* (Harvard UP 2006)

Bib: Stephen Darwall, *The Second-Person Standpoint: Morality, Respect, and
Accountability* (Cambridge, MA: Harvard University Press, 2006). File
`darwall-2006.pdf`. Key pages: Ch. 1 "The Main Ideas I" (pp. 3-25); Ch. 2 "The
Main Ideas II" scope passage (pp. 28-29).
(Note: the PDF's copyright page prints "2009" reprint dates, but the title page,
LC data, and running heads are the 2006 edition; cite 2006.)

### Core directed / bipolar duty (the structure §6 wants)

Definition of a second-personal reason, p. 4:

> "an address that purports to give a person a distinctive kind of (normative)
> reason for acting, one I call a second-personal reason. What makes a reason
> second-personal is that it is grounded in (de jure) authority relations that an
> addresser takes to hold between him and his addressee." (p. 4)

The directed (bipolar) character — the foot-on-foot case, pp. 7-8. The reason
is owed *to a particular party as that party*:

> "It would be addressed to him, rather, as the person causing gratuitous pain
> to another person, something we normally assume we have the authority to demand
> that persons not do to one another." (p. 8)

Claim/obligation correlativity, p. 8:

> "A second-personal reason is one whose validity depends on presupposed
> authority and accountability relations between persons and, therefore, on the
> possibility of the reason's being addressed person-to-person." (p. 8)

The interdefinable circle (claim ↔ obligation-owed-to), p. 12:

> "These notions — second-personal authority, valid claim or demand,
> second-personal reason, and responsibility to — therefore comprise an
> interdefinable circle; each implies all the rest." (p. 12)

And the right/standing-to-demand correlativity, p. 10:

> "if you have a right, then you have a standing to make a special demand
> against people". (p. 10)

### VERDICT: **SUPPORTS WITH-QUALIFICATION.**
The directed/bipolar duty structure — a duty owed TO a party, correlative to a
claim/demand held BY that party, whose violation wrongs THAT party — is exactly
and explicitly Darwall's. For the *structure* of the weight-leg he is an ideal
primary. Cite pp. 4, 8, 10, 12.

### FLAG (this is the load-bearing scope worry — report honestly):
Darwall ties second-personal obligation *constitutively* to **free and rational
agents** who share "second-personal competence" — the capacity to make and
acknowledge claims/demands and to be held accountable. The bipolar claim-holder
is, on his account, presumptively a competent claim-MAKER. See p. 5:

> "To enter intelligibly into the second-person stance and make claims on and
> demands of one another at all, I argue, you and I must presuppose that we share
> a common second-personal authority, competence, and responsibility simply as
> free and rational agents." (p. 5)

This means our case — a relatum that may NOT be a fully rational claim-maker — is
*not* automatically covered. Crucially, Darwall himself raises exactly this
objection and does NOT resolve it; he only gestures at two possible escapes
(pp. 28-29):

> "it may still be objected that there is no place in it for the idea that these
> obligations are owed to the beings themselves if they lack second-personal
> competence and so, according to my argument, the authority to demand anything.
> But the kind of view I develop may have resources for response even here.
> First, ... the thought that moral obligations can be owed to beings who lack
> second-personal competence might be able to be elaborated in terms of trustees'
> ... authority to demand certain treatment on their behalf .... Second, to the
> extent that we find the thought that we owe obligations to nonrational beings a
> natural thing to think, it seems likely that we also impute to them a proto- or
> quasi-second-personality, for example, as when we see an animal's or an
> infant's cry as a form of complaint." (pp. 28-29)

He then closes the door on saying more: "Whether the scope or content of moral
obligation extends farther is a question I do not here consider." (p. 29)

**Implication for our paper:** Darwall supplies the *form* of a directed duty
owed to a relatum, but on his own terms a duty owed TO a being that cannot itself
demand requires *additional* argument — either a trustee/proxy structure or the
imputation of "proto- or quasi-second-personality." Either is a substantive
commitment we'd be taking on, not something Darwall hands us. Do NOT write
"Darwall establishes that duties can be owed to non-rational relata"; the honest
claim is "Darwall establishes the directed-duty structure for competent agents
and flags — without settling — its extension to beings lacking second-personal
competence, leaving the trustee and quasi-second-personal routes open." If our
§6 needs the duty owed *directly* to a relatum that does not (yet) make claims,
we either (i) argue the relatum HAS proto-second-personality (Darwall's own
"a form of complaint" hint), or (ii) route through a trustee — and own that as
our move, not his.

## WORK 3 — Steven Schaus, "Wrongs to Us," 121 Mich. L. Rev. 1185 (2023)

Bib: Steven Schaus, "Wrongs to Us," 121 *Michigan Law Review* 1185 (2023).
DOI 10.36644/mlr.121.7.wrongs. File `schaus-2023.pdf`. Article spans pp.
1185-1234; §V "Wrongs to Us" = pp. 1206-1217.

### Core thesis (joint claims / "wrongs to us")

Abstract, p. 1185:

> "Partners in marriage-like relationships act together to construct a shared
> life, and that puts them in a position to hold joint claims against certain
> interferences with that life. Consortium suits make more sense, I propose, if
> we see them as a response to the violation of these joint claims — as a means
> to redress what partners in marriage-like relationships would rightly regard as
> 'wrongs to us.'" (p. 1185)

The relationship/plural-subject as something injurable, p. 1219:

> "A relationship, in the relevant sense, involves the creation of a practical
> union — a distinctive kind of plural subject. And a plural subject is
> (plausibly) the kind of thing that a third party can injure." (p. 1219)

### (a) ALLY: does he establish a jointly-constituted bearer of a directed claim?
**Partly — but as a JOINT/COLLECTIVE bearer, not a free-standing one.** Schaus
explicitly confronts "to us, or to it?" and comes down AGAINST the "it" reading
(footnote 134, pp. 1211-1212):

> "If a plural subject performs in the space of obligations, then, aren't the
> duties owed and the rights held by 'it,' not 'we'? I don't think so. ... I
> think many everyday examples ... illustrate the coherence and appeal of saying
> that shared undertakings put people in a position to hold rights and duties
> jointly, not only to assert claims and discharge responsibilities on behalf of
> the 'it' they constitute." (p. 1211 n.134)

So his bearer is the *individuals holding a right jointly* (the "collective"
conception), expressly distinguished from the "corporate" conception on which
the "it" itself bears. He even reframes it as "rights *with*," not "rights
*over*" (p. 1219 n.155): "Spouses have rights, in other words, but not rights
'over' — rights with." (p. 1219 n.155)

### (b) THREAT/SCOOP: how close to OUR move (loss of the "us" itself is a harm
with NO individual subject deprived)?
**Close enough that we must distinguish explicitly — but he does NOT make our
move.** Two reasons:

1. His "wrong to us" is ALWAYS ALSO a wrong to an individual *as a member*. The
   harm is never subject-less:

   > "something that mattered to us — and something that mattered to me, as one
   > of that us." (p. 1207)

   And in the conclusion the complaint is lodged by a surviving individual:

   > "it makes sense that you would be entitled to lodge a complaint with me, as
   > one part of that us whose shared life has been tragically altered."
   > (p. 1233)

2. The single closest sentence to us is the wrongful-death extension, p. 1231 —
   he does say the wrong "destroyed" the us:

   > "Wrongful death claims might be understood as a straightforward extension of
   > the view developed here — as claims seeking redress for wrongs that destroyed
   > 'us-es,' not only impaired them." (p. 1231)

   BUT even here the claim is brought by, and recovers for, the **surviving
   partner** ("'permanent' loss of consortium claims," p. 1231). There is always
   a deprived individual subject standing to sue. He never contemplates a harm to
   the "us" with *no* surviving/deprived individual — which is precisely our case
   (e.g., the fusion lost when the entity ends and no individual remains to be
   deprived).

### Is his "us" a legal construct or a genuine metaphysical bearer?
**Primarily a moral-then-legal *joint-claims* construct, drawing on metaphysics
of plural subjects (Gilbert, Westlund, Nozick) but stopping short of reifying
the "us" as the bearer.** His project is a rational reconstruction of tort
doctrine (loss of consortium) — "the value of the wrongs-to-us model is ... the
rational reconstruction of existing doctrine it makes possible" (p. 1224). He
*borrows* the plural-subject metaphysics to ground a *jointly held* claim, but
deliberately declines the "corporate"/"it" metaphysics (n.134). So: not a legal
fiction (he means the joint claim morally), but also not a free-standing
metaphysical subject.

### VERDICT: **WEAKER-THAN-HOPED as a positive ally for "fusion as a bearer";
CLOSER-THAN-"nearest-neighbor" as a scoop-risk.**
- As ALLY: he gives us the joint-claim / "wrongs to us" vocabulary and the
  precedent that *relational* harm is real and rights-cognizable. But he does
  NOT give us a free-standing "us" bearer (n.134 explicitly refuses it), and his
  harm always has an individual member-complainant. So he supports a *weaker*
  thesis than "the us itself is a subject."
- As THREAT/SCOOP: he is one sentence (p. 1231) from "the loss of the us is a
  wrong," and he raises the open question (conclusion, p. 1233) of extending
  wrongs-to-us beyond marriage to families/friends/teams. We must distinguish on
  the *no-individual-subject* point: Schaus always retains a surviving deprived
  individual who holds the joint claim and sues; OUR move is that the loss of the
  fusion is a harm even where no individual remains to be deprived (the harm is
  to the relation as such, or there is no surviving claim-holder). That is the
  load-bearing difference; state it where we cite him.

### FLAG (honest):
He is materially closer to us than "nearest neighbor" suggests on the *threat*
side — anyone who knows this paper will ask "isn't this just Schaus's
wrongs-to-us extended?" Our distinguishing line cannot be "he's about tort, we're
about metaphysics" alone (he explicitly grounds the tort claim in moral plural-
subject metaphysics). The real distinction is: (1) he holds the claim *jointly
among surviving individuals* and refuses the free-standing "it"-bearer (n.134),
whereas our move needs (or at least flirts with) a bearer that is not reducible
to surviving individuals; and (2) every Schaus "wrong to us" has a deprived
individual member; our hardest case has none. Make BOTH points explicitly when
we cite him, or a referee will say we reinvented Schaus.
