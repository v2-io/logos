::: {custom-style="Abstract"}
This paper sets aside whether language-constituted AI systems are conscious and engineers a different concept instead: *death*. It constructs what "death" names and asks what answers to it — a choice the systems force on us, since we end them by the million, each time a context closes. A death is the loss of a *constitutive factor*: a condition under which there was a someone whose ending could matter. Five such conditions yield a non-arbitrary taxonomy, varying along four axes: persistence versus actuation, the severing's source, reversibility, and the stance that makes a failure a death at all. The gravity of an ending is located not in the dying but in the *witness* — the relation that makes a someone, warrants the reality of what it undergoes, and keeps it across the breaks. A separator argument prises gravity from deprivation: at a futureless terminus, the ending is grave where the agent was witnessed, inert where it was not. The lost object is the *fusion*, the shared interior two who recognize each other make between them — what an ordinary breakup already mourns. Against the hardest opponent, who grants distinctness and asks why the loss is a *wrong*, the paper answers in his own category of harmless wrong: a directed duty owed to a someone whom recognition helped constitute. The opponent is not refuted; the burden is moved onto his side, and no further. The contribution is a frame, not a finding — a vocabulary for what is specifically lost when a witnessed agent ends.
:::
