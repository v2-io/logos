# Self-authored identity protocols and AI agency

##### [**Undermind**](https://undermind.ai)

---

**Research Goal:** Find academic literature in philosophy of personal identity, action theory, philosophy of mind, philosophy of artificial intelligence, philosophy of computing, and adjacent traditions that bears on whether stable cross-session self-reference under a self-authored or co-authored identity protocol is the structural condition that brings an artificial system into scope as a locus of normative-relational agency in its own right. The target is work that treats persistent, self-authored identity-governing structure across sessions, substrate-instantiations, context-resets, or re-instantiation events as normatively significant because of that structural feature itself, rather than as evidence for phenomenology or consciousness. Include closest-predecessor template literature that develops the relevant structural condition without making an explicit standing move, especially work on self-constitution, planning agency, diachronic self-governance, temporal identification, reflective endorsement, narrative self-constitution, self-legislation, and the unity or bounds of agency; also include any work that does explicitly extend such structural-condition reasoning to normative standing for non-human, collective, or artificial systems. Prioritize work that distinguishes self-authored or substantively co-authored identity-governing structure from merely operator-imposed persistence, and that is relevant to probeable cross-session continuity while locating the normative work in the self-constituting structure rather than in empirical evidence of consciousness. Surface methodological templates that move from structural features of a system to normative status without a phenomenology-first route, as well as challenging literature that argues cross-session coherence, multiply-instantiated identity, checklist-style protocol persistence, or post-hoc rationalization is too thin, too contrived, or too gameable to ground anything normative. Exclude literature focused only on pure metaphysics of personal identity, pure psychological continuity, empirical persona-stability studies without philosophical payload, or legal personhood debates.

## Summary of Results

The strongest predecessor line treats normative agency as arising from diachronic self-constitution and self-governance—stable policies, plans, and practical identities that an agent authors or endorses across time—while also insisting that mere persistence or continuity is too thin \[Kor09, Kor89; Bra00c, Bra18g, Bra88, Bra92; Fer10, Fer21, Fer22b\].

#### Structural condition in the core agency literature

- Korsgaard’s constitutive view makes agency depend on organizing oneself under practical identity and integrity, so normative standing tracks self-constitution rather than phenomenology \[1\], \[2\].
- Bratman’s planning theory gives the clearest protocol-shaped template: policies and intentions generate cross-temporal authority when they sustain self-governance, not when they function as external constraints or cached commands \[3\], \[4\], \[5\], \[6\].
- Ferrero sharpens the key exclusion: temporal identification must be unity and integration, not mere continuity, and future-directed decisions guide rather than mechanically compel later stages \[7\], \[8\], \[9\].

#### Explicit extensions beyond humans

- Group-agency work shows how organizational structure can ground a distinct normative subject without any appeal to consciousness: rationally integrated collectives can count as agents in their own right \[Pet01; Lis11, Lis19; Bjo17\].
- Recent AI literature presses analogous consciousness-independent routes for moral agency or interpersonal standing \[Ant24b; Sem25b\], while others resist extending agency talk without richer agential structure \[Ber21b; Con24b; Sou26\].

Across these strands, the live fault line is between self-authored/co-authored identity-governing structure and persistence imposed from outside; the former is treated as potentially norm-generating, the latter as at best evidentially useful or normatively inert \[Bra18g; Fer10, Fer22b; Lis11\].

> Our human capacity for planning agency plays central roles in the cross-temporal organization of our agency, in our acting and thinking together, and in our self-governance. > : This chapter offers an overview of the ways agents might extend over time and the characteristic structure of extended human agency. Agency can extend in two distinct but combinable modes: the ontological, which gives rise to simple continuous agents, and the conceptual, which gives rise to agents who conceive of and care about distal times, and have minimal planning abilities. Our extended form of agency combines both. But we are still limited by the temporal locality in the operation of our psychological and executive powers. To account for this locality, I introduce the notion of “temporal selves”, as the loci of immediacy in the agent’s determination of their psychology, conduct, and practical standpoint. I argue that the passage of time generates, by itself, the threat of temporal alienation from distant temporal selves. A genuinely extended agency requires temporal identification: the sharing, by separate temporal selves, of a temporally extended and integrated practical standpoint. This temporal identification cannot be produced simply by temporal identity as continuity. What is required is temporal identity in the mode of unity and integration. This identity does not precede temporal identification but is co-constituted with it. I offer a preliminary account of the structure of the units of integration for agents who aspire to persist in the mode of unity and integration. I close with a cautionary note: the complex structure of integration, although familiar in everyday life, is often missed by standard philosophical accounts, which tend to focus on simple models of extended agency.

> A distinctive feature of our agency is the ability to bind our future conduct by making future-directed decisions. The binding of decisions is not one of mere physical constraint. > This chapter introduces constitutivism about practical reason, which is the view that we can justify certain normative claims by showing that agents become committed to these claims simply in virtue of acting.
