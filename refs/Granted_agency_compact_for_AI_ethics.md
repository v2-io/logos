# Granted agency compact for AI ethics

##### [**Undermind**](https://undermind.ai)

---

**Research Goal:** Find academic papers that can scaffold a philosophical account of AI ethics as a granted-agency compact: a structurally bilateral ethical relation between asymmetrically comprehending parties, where both retain some real agency, refusal, and the possibility of bad faith, and where the relation is framed as compact, contract, treaty, mutual obligation, mutual recognition, or granted-but-recognized sovereignty rather than primarily as rights-attribution, welfare-attribution, or tool-management. Prioritize work that articulates the structural form of such an asymmetric but bilateral relation, especially papers that explicitly reconstruct or extend a form from one domain to another in a way that is citable for methodological support. Be permissive about partial analogues if they illuminate the form. Include three layers of material: (1) the closest predecessors in AI ethics that treat human–AI relations as compact-like, contractual, treaty-like, or mutual-recognition structures; (2) adjacent non-AI traditions that articulate comparable forms under asymmetry, including developmental ethics, Eastern philosophy, political and legal theory, fiduciary and proxy-decision ethics, care ethics, civil-disobedience theory, sovereignty and non-domination, ethics under asymmetric power, and related work where both parties retain standing to refuse or act in bad faith despite unequal capacity; and (3) strong contrary positions arguing that this compact-form is the wrong shape for AI ethics even under asymmetric uncertainty. Exclude generic AI ethics surveys, AI safety / x-risk, pure engineering governance, pure welfare-extension arguments without engagement with structural form, and legal-personhood debates unless they directly bear on the compact-form question.

## Summary of Results

The literature contains no settled AI-ethics tradition that treats human–AI relations as a bilateral compact between asymmetrically capable parties; the closest citable foundations instead converge from social- and incomplete-contract framings \[1\], \[2\], legal agency and electronic contracting \[3\], \[4\], \[5\], \[6\], and HRI work on mutual recognition, commitment, consent, and refusal \[7\], \[8\], \[9\], \[10\].

#### Closest AI-side predecessors

- **Compact mostly appears as governance among humans around AI**, not yet as a human–AI bilateral form: algorithmic social contract \[1\], sociotechnical commitments and dissent channels \[11\], and participatory/contestable service-agreement proposals \[12\], \[13\].
- **Contract doctrine supplies the strongest structural vocabulary for asymmetry plus agency**: incomplete contracts and external gap-fillers \[2\]; limited legal agency for artificial agents \[3\]; objective consent, good faith, and fair dealing in electronic contracting \[4\], \[5\], \[14\].
- **Bilateral interaction norms appear in thinner HRI form**: mutual recognition \[7\], commitment management \[8\], \[15\], consent as a governing norm \[9\], and disobedience/refusal as retained agency \[10\], \[16\].

#### Adjacent scaffolding

- **Fiduciary and loyalty traditions** model asymmetric but non-purely-instrumental relations: AI loyalty \[17\], Fiduciary AI \[18\], \[19\], information fiduciaries and loyalty/trust \[20\], \[21\], \[22\].
- **Republican anti-domination work** clarifies when asymmetry becomes illegitimate unless contestable: \[23\], \[24\], \[25\], \[26\], \[27\], \[28\], \[29\].

#### Strong contrary line

- Several papers argue the compact shape fails because present AI lacks robust reciprocity or answerability: reciprocity in social robots is structurally deceptive \[30\]; partnership claims overstate current AI capacities \[31\], \[32\]; provider-controlled AI relations are triadic and normatively hollow rather than bilateral \[33\].

By sorting various kinds of consent through social and legal doctrine, we delineate empirical and technical questions to meet consent challenges faced in major application domains and robotic roles. Attention to consent could show, for example, how extraordinary, norm-violating actions can be justified by agents and accepted by those around them. We argue that operationalizing ideas from legal scholarship can better guide how robotic systems might cultivate and sustain proper forms of consent.

**Algorithmic Contracts** ([link](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/da516646527f9f036eca0dbd93f4a3d5a8497455))\
*ERN: Economics of Contract: Theory (Topic)* · Oct 1, 2016 · 35 citations

> Algorithmic contracts are contracts in which an algorithm determines a party’s obligations. Some contracts are algorithmic because the parties used algorithms as negotiators before contract formation, choosing which terms to offer or accept. Other contracts are algorithmic because the parties agree that an algorithm to be run at some time after the contract is formed will serve as a gap-filler. Such agreements are already common in high speed trading of financial products and will soon spread to other contexts. However, contract law doctrine does not currently have a coherent approach to describing the creation and enforcement of algorithmic contracts. This Article fills this significant gap in doctrinal law and legal literature, providing a definition and novel taxonomy of algorithmic contracts. The algorithmic contracts that present the most significant problems for contract law are those that involve “black box” algorithmic agents, whose decision-making is not functionally understandable ex ante – or sometimes even not human-intelligible at all. There is only a tenuous case for their enforceability under currently accepted approaches to contract law. The Uniform Electronic Transactions Act (UETA) was written and widely adopted nearly twenty years ago to make sure that contracts made electronically using basic automation techniques would be recognized as enforceable. However, the language of the UETA may be read to treat all putative contracts made with algorithms as properly formed, simply because they happen to be electronic. Unintended consequences of this approach include opportunities for fraud, market manipulation, and a general lack of algorithmic accountability. This Article’s approach looks to the common law of agency for inspiration. Some algorithms commonly used in contract formation have been delegated a level of responsibility that justifies the use of agency principles. Algorithms should be considered constructive agents for the purpose of contract formation. The company consenting to the contract can be said to have authorized or ratified the contract formed on its behalf by the algorithm. This approach explains easy cases while also showing why algorithmic contracts, even many black box algorithmic contracts, are enforceable. Furthermore, establishing a doctrinally robust connection between the actions of the algorithm and the intent of the contracting party promotes algorithmic accountability.
